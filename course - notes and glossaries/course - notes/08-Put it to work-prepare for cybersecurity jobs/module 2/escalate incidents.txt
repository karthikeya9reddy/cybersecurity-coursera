Topics: Incident escalation, incident classification, escalation policies, security team responsibilities, incident criticality, breach notification, and incident prioritization.
1. What Is Incident Escalation?
Security incident escalation is the process of identifying a potential security incident, triaging it, and, when appropriate, transferring it to a more experienced team member or responsible department.

Escalation ensures security issues are investigated and handled by the appropriate personnel before they become more serious.
Why escalation is important
- Prevents minor security issues from becoming major incidents.
- Reduces the risk of sensitive data exposure.
- Helps minimize operational and financial damage.
- Ensures incidents reach the appropriate response teams.
- Supports compliance with organizational policies and applicable laws.
Important: Not every security event must be escalated. Analysts should follow their organization's escalation policy.
2. Incident Escalation Process
1. Identify
Detect suspicious activity through alerts, logs, or monitoring
2. Triage
Assess the incident type, affected assets, and potential impact
3. Prioritize
Determine the appropriate urgency and criticality
4. Escalate if required
Notify the responsible team according to company policy
5. Document
Record the findings, actions, and notifications

Two essential analyst skills
1. Attention to detail: Identify unusual activity, suspicious patterns, and potential threats.
2. Following escalation procedures: Know who to notify, when to escalate, and which communication channels to use.
3. Escalation Policy
An escalation policy is a set of documented procedures describing how security issues should be reported and handled.

It typically specifies:
- Which security events require escalation.
- Who should receive notifications.
- How to communicate incidents.
- Who to contact if the primary responder is unavailable.
- How quickly different incidents should be escalated.
Example
An organization may require escalation when an account experiences 10 failed login attempts.

An analyst identifies the activity in authentication logs, reviews the company's escalation policy, and notifies the password protection team.

Remember: Escalation thresholds differ between organizations.
4. Three Important Security Incident Types
A. Malware infection
A malware infection occurs when malicious software infiltrates a computer or network.

Examples include ransomware and other malicious programs.

Possible consequences:
- System performance degradation.
- Data theft or corruption.
- Operational disruption.
- Loss of access to important information.
B. Unauthorized access
Unauthorized access occurs when someone gains physical or digital access to a system, application, or data without permission.

Examples:
- Stolen credentials used to access accounts.
- Successful brute-force attacks.
- Access to restricted databases.
- Unauthorized physical access to protected systems.
C. Improper usage
Improper usage occurs when an employee violates an organization's acceptable use policies.

Examples:
- Installing unauthorized applications.
- Using company software licenses for personal purposes.
- Accessing another employee's information without authorization.
Improper usage may be intentional or accidental. The course recommends escalating these incidents to a supervisor rather than assuming intent.
Incident types comparison
Incident type	Main concern	Example
Malware infection	Malicious software	Ransomware on a workstation
Unauthorized access	Access without permission	Compromised employee credentials
Improper usage	Policy violation	Installing prohibited software

Technical distinction: Phishing is generally a social-engineering attack, not itself a malware infection, although phishing messages can deliver malware.
5. Incident Criticality and Prioritization
Incident criticality describes the seriousness of an incident and its potential impact on organizational assets or operations.

The course introduces three broad levels.
Criticality	Description	Example
Low	Limited apparent impact	Isolated failed login from a known employee
Medium	Potential risk requiring further investigation	Suspicious activity involving an unapproved application
High	Significant potential impact on critical operations or sensitive data	Unauthorized access to a customer financial database

These examples are illustrative. Actual severity depends on the evidence and the organization's policy.
What determines incident priority?
The course emphasizes the importance of the affected asset.

A threat targeting a manufacturing control application or a database storing customer PII may require greater urgency than an isolated failed login.

Other relevant factors include incident scope, potential damage, available evidence, and business importance.
Criticality can change
An incident may initially receive a medium classification because information is limited.

After further investigation, an experienced incident handler may increase or decrease its criticality.

Key lesson: Prioritization is dynamic. Analysts should update their assessment as new evidence becomes available.

6. Roles in the Escalation Process
Different security and data-management roles handle different responsibilities.
Role	Main responsibility	When they may be involved
Data owner	Determines authorized access and use of organizational information	Unauthorized access to owned data
Data controller	Determines why and how personal data is processed	Risks involving customer personal information
Data processor	Processes personal data on behalf of a controller	Third-party data-processing security issues
Data custodian	Implements and manages data protection controls and access	Compromised permissions or access controls
Data Protection Officer (DPO)	Monitors and advises on data protection compliance	Privacy policy or regulatory concerns

Additional organizational roles
Depending on the incident, other teams may participate:
- CISO: Oversees organizational information security.
- Security engineers and incident responders: Investigate and remediate technical issues.
- Legal team: Advises on legal obligations and regulatory concerns.
- Public relations or communications team: Manages appropriate external communication.
Responsibilities and reporting structures vary between organizations.
7. Breach Notification Requirements
Some security breaches trigger legal obligations to notify affected individuals or regulatory authorities.

These obligations may apply to compromised PII and other protected information.

Examples include personal identification numbers, addresses, financial details, and medical records.

Important: Notification requirements vary by jurisdiction, data type, and breach circumstances. Not every security incident automatically triggers a legal reporting obligation.

As an entry-level analyst, your responsibility is generally to escalate potential breaches through the organization's approved process, rather than independently deciding legal notifications.
8. Consequences of Delayed Escalation
Even apparently minor incidents may develop into serious problems when left uninvestigated.
Course example: Unauthorized application activity
An analyst notices unusual log activity involving an application recently banned by the organization.

Instead of escalating promptly, the analyst postpones reporting it.

Later, the company experiences a serious breach affecting manufacturing operations. The security team discovers that the incident originated from suspicious activity involving the banned application.

Possible consequences include:
- Manufacturing downtime.
- Financial losses.
- Compromised data.
- Disruption of business operations.
- Reputational damage.
Key lesson: Delayed escalation can allow attackers or security problems to persist and expand.

9. Case Study — Juliana Soto
The course presents a scenario involving Juliana Soto, a new cybersecurity analyst at the fictional Right-On-Time Payment Solutions.
Two simultaneous security alerts

Alert 1 — Ten failed login attempts

An employee account is locked after 10 failed logins. The organization's policy requires notification of the password protection team.




Alert 2 — Attempted compromise of financial database

An unknown source attempts to compromise a system containing customer bank account information, potentially affecting hundreds of customers.



Juliana's decision
She prioritizes the attempted compromise of the customer financial database because it threatens a highly sensitive asset.

She follows the escalation policy, notifies the appropriate team, and then escalates the failed-login event.
What this teaches
- Incidents must be prioritized according to risk and asset importance.
- Multiple alerts may require different levels of urgency.
- Escalation guidelines help analysts make consistent decisions.
- Lower-priority incidents should still receive appropriate attention.

10. Technical SOC Analyst Example
Imagine a SOC analyst sees the following simplified authentication records:
10:01 admin login_failed 192.0.2.10
10:02 admin login_failed 192.0.2.10
10:03 admin login_failed 192.0.2.10
10:04 admin login_failed 192.0.2.10
10:05 admin login_success 192.0.2.10


The sequence shows four failed attempts followed by a successful login.
Initial investigation
An analyst should examine whether the activity is consistent with normal user behavior or possible unauthorized access.

Useful questions include:
- Is the source IP expected for this account?
- Were the attempts generated by the legitimate user?
- Does the organization have a threshold for failed logins?
- Is the account privileged?
- Did suspicious activity occur after the successful login?
A suspicious pattern does not automatically prove compromise.
Escalation decision
If the activity meets the organization's defined escalation criteria, the analyst should notify the appropriate response team and provide relevant evidence.
Example incident report fields
Field	Example
Alert type	Multiple failed logins followed by success
Affected account	admin
Source IP	192.0.2.10
Time range	10:01–10:05
Potential risk	Unauthorized access
Status	Investigation required
Escalation	According to company policy

Five essential rules
1. Not every event needs escalation, but every relevant alert should be handled according to policy.
2. Prioritize incidents based on affected assets and potential impact.
3. Malware infection, unauthorized access, and improper usage are important incident classifications.
4. Delayed escalation can turn minor security issues into major incidents.
5. When unsure, consult the escalation policy and ask the appropriate supervisor or team.
