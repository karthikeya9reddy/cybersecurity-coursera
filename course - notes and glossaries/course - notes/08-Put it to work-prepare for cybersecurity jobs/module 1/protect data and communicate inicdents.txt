Cybersecurity Notes — Security Mindset, Asset Protection & Business Continuity
Google Cybersecurity Professional Certificate · Course 8: Put It to Work — Prepare for Cybersecurity Jobs

Topics: Security mindset, data classification, asset classification, incident escalation, business continuity planning, disaster recovery, and information security lifecycle.
1. Security Mindset
A security mindset is the ability to evaluate risks and continuously identify potential or actual security breaches involving systems, applications, and data.

Security professionals must understand:
- Assets: What needs protection?
- Threats: What could harm those assets?
- Vulnerabilities: What weaknesses could be exploited?
- Risks: What are the potential consequences of threats exploiting vulnerabilities?
Why a security mindset matters
A security mindset helps analysts:
- Identify suspicious activities before they develop into serious incidents.
- Understand the importance of organizational assets.
- Stay informed about emerging threats and vulnerabilities.
- Evaluate potential risks before making decisions.
- Protect customer information and business operations.
Example: An employee installs an unauthorized application on a company computer. A security analyst should recognize that this could introduce vulnerabilities or malicious software and report the concern according to company procedures.
2. Data Classification
Data classification is the process of categorizing information according to its sensitivity and protection requirements.

The course introduces four data classifications.
Classification	Description	Examples
Public	Information available to the general public	Press releases, job postings, marketing materials
Private	Information not intended for public access	Employee IDs, internal email addresses, research data
Sensitive	Information requiring protection against unauthorized access	PII, SPII, PHI, banking details, passwords
Confidential	Restricted information important to business operations	Trade secrets, financial records, proprietary information

Important data types
PII — Personally Identifiable Information

Information that can identify an individual, such as their name, address, or identification number.

SPII — Sensitive Personally Identifiable Information

Highly sensitive personal information, such as passport numbers, government identification numbers, or financial account details.

PHI — Protected Health Information

Individually identifiable health information protected under applicable healthcare privacy rules, particularly HIPAA in the United States.

NDA — Non-Disclosure Agreement

A legal agreement requiring parties to protect confidential information.

Technical clarification: Organizations may use different classification schemes. Private, sensitive, and confidential are not universally fixed levels in one hierarchy; actual handling requirements depend on organizational policy and applicable law.
3. Asset Classification
Asset classification involves categorizing organizational assets according to their importance, value, and sensitivity.
Low-level vs. high-level assets
Low-level assets	High-level assets
Generally lower sensitivity	Higher sensitivity or business importance
Publicly accessible information	Restricted or critical information
Public marketing materials	Customer financial information
Public website address	Trade secrets and intellectual property

High-level asset compromise can result in financial losses, reputational damage, reduced customer trust, and legal or regulatory consequences.

Remember: A publicly accessible asset can still be business-critical. For example, a public website may contain no confidential information but can be essential to business operations.
4. Security Incidents and Escalation
A security event is an observable occurrence within a system or network.

A security incident involves activity that compromises or threatens organizational security.

Incident escalation is the process of reporting a suspected or confirmed security issue to the appropriate supervisor, incident response team, or security personnel.
Examples requiring evaluation
Situation	Potential concern
Employee installs unauthorized software	Unapproved application or potential vulnerability
Suspicious code execution appears in logs	Possible malware or unauthorized activity
Sensitive customer data is exposed	Confidentiality breach
Multiple suspicious authentication attempts occur	Possible credential attack

Incident escalation process
1. Detect suspicious activity
Identify an unusual event or possible security violation
2. Assess the situation
Review available evidence and possible impact
3. Report or escalate
Notify the appropriate security personnel
4. Document the findings
Record relevant observations and follow established procedures

Important: Do not ignore suspicious activity simply because its impact initially appears small. Follow the organization's escalation policy.

A confirmed data breach is one type of security incident, but not every security incident requires confirmed data exposure.
5. Protecting Organizational Assets
The course describes a four-part approach to protecting organizations.
1. Identify assets
Determine which systems and information need protection
2. Identify threats
Determine what could negatively affect those assets
3. Implement detection
Use security tools and monitoring processes
4. Prepare BCP and DRP
Plan how operations and systems will recover from disruption

6. Business Continuity Plan (BCP)
A Business Continuity Plan documents how an organization will maintain essential business operations during and after a significant disruption.
Four important BCP steps
1. Business Impact Analysis (BIA): Determine how disruptions could affect business processes.
2. Recovery procedures: Document the steps needed to maintain or restore critical functions.
3. Business continuity team: Assign responsibilities to personnel from departments such as IT, security, HR, communications, and operations.
4. Training and exercises: Prepare teams through scenarios and testing.
Example
If a payment company's primary infrastructure becomes unavailable, the BCP describes how the organization will continue essential payment-related operations, communicate with customers, and coordinate its response.
7. Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)
A Disaster Recovery Plan describes how an organization restores affected technology, systems, applications, and data after a serious disruption.

The course emphasizes:
- Restoring software functionality.
- Recovering affected hardware and infrastructure.
- Identifying impacted applications and data.
- Minimizing disruption caused by security incidents.
Example: Ransomware incident
If ransomware makes important business systems unavailable, a disaster recovery plan helps guide restoration of those systems and data.
BCP vs. DRP
BCP	DRP
Maintains business operations	Restores affected technology
Focuses on organizational continuity	Focuses on technical recovery
Involves multiple business departments	Usually involves IT, security, and recovery specialists
Addresses how work continues	Addresses how systems are restored

Remember: BCP and DRP support each other. Disaster recovery is an important component of broader business continuity planning.
8. Information Security Lifecycle
The course presents an information lifecycle strategy with four steps.
Stage	Purpose
1. Identify	Identify important assets and sensitive information
2. Assess	Review security controls, policies, vulnerabilities, and procedures
3. Protect	Implement safeguards for organizational assets
4. Monitor	Monitor security processes and systems continuously

Case study: Payment processing company
The fictional company Right-On-Time Payment Solutions processes customer payments.

The new security analyst, Juliana Soto, discovers that the company handles customer names, usernames, passwords, identification information, and banking details.

She follows the information lifecycle:
- Identify: Recognize sensitive customer and financial information as critical assets.
- Assess: Review security policies and existing protective controls.
- Protect: Observe how experienced analysts safeguard company systems.
- Monitor: Work with her supervisor to understand security monitoring procedures.
Key lesson: Before protecting an organization, an analyst must understand what information and systems are most important to its operations.

Five essential rules
1. Know which assets need protection and what threatens them.
2. Classify information before deciding how it should be handled.
3. Report suspicious activity according to organizational escalation procedures.
4. Use BCP to maintain business operations and DRP to restore technology.
5. Continuously assess and monitor the security of critical assets.
