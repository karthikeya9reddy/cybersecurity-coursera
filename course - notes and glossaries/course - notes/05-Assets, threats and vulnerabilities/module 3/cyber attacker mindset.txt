1. Attack surface and security hardening
An attack surface is the collection of potential entry points and weaknesses that a threat actor could exploit.
Organizations have two main attack surfaces:
Attack surface	Includes	Example
Physical	People and their devices	An unattended laptop displaying confidential information
Digital	Systems and resources connected online	Cloud services, servers, websites, and network services
Cloud computing expands the digital attack surface because organizational resources can be accessed from outside the traditional office network.
Security hardening means strengthening a system to reduce vulnerabilities and minimize the number of possible entry points.
Examples include:
- Applying access controls and organizational policies.
- Restricting unnecessary access.
- Securing unattended devices and sensitive information.
- Removing unnecessary services and entry points.
Key principle: The smaller the attack surface, the easier it is to protect.
2. Applying an attacker mindset
An attacker mindset means examining a system from an attacker's perspective to identify weaknesses and improve defenses.
Proactive vs reactive simulations
Proactive simulation	Reactive simulation
Takes the attacker's perspective	Takes the defender's perspective
Attempts to exploit weaknesses in an authorized exercise	Investigates weaknesses and determines how to respond
Associated with red-team exercises	Associated with blue-team exercises
Reactive exercises commonly use vulnerability scans to identify weaknesses and prioritize remediation.
Vulnerability assessment example
1. Identification: Discover a server running an outdated operating system.
2. Vulnerability analysis: Research the operating system's known weaknesses.
3. Risk assessment: Evaluate severity, impact, and likelihood of exploitation.
4. Remediation: Patch, update, or otherwise address the vulnerability.
Security professionals should document and communicate their findings clearly. The course recommends the NIST National Vulnerability Database as a resource for staying informed: NVD website.
3. Threat actors
A threat actor is a person or group that presents a security risk, whether intentionally or accidentally.
Five threat actor categories
Category	Meaning
Competitors	Rival companies that might benefit from stolen or leaked information
State actors	Government intelligence agencies or state-sponsored actors
Criminal syndicates	Organized groups that profit from criminal activities
Insider threats	Current or former authorized individuals who accidentally or deliberately put resources at risk
Shadow IT	Use of technologies outside established IT governance
Shadow IT example: An employee uses a personal email account to send confidential company information without approval.
Three types of hackers
- Unauthorized or malicious hackers: Access systems without authorization to commit harmful or criminal acts.
- Authorized or ethical hackers: Test systems with permission to improve security.
- Semi-authorized hackers: May violate ethical standards without necessarily intending conventional criminal harm; hacktivists may exploit systems to promote political or social causes.
Script kiddies are attackers who rely on prewritten code and tools rather than developing their own sophisticated attack methods.
Advanced persistent threats (APTs)
An advanced persistent threat is a situation in which a threat actor maintains unauthorized access to a system for an extended period.
- Often associated with state-sponsored actors.
- May involve long-term surveillance and intelligence gathering.
- Can target private organizations as a stepping stone toward larger organizations or government entities.
4. Attack vectors — pathways into a system
An attack vector is a pathway or method used to gain unauthorized access to a system or exploit a weakness.
The course identifies seven categories:
Attack vector	Example
Direct access	An attacker physically accesses an unattended computer
Removable media	A malicious or compromised USB drive
Social media	Sensitive company information is exposed in a post
Email	A malicious link or phishing email
Wireless networks	An attacker targets an insecure wireless network
Cloud services	A weakness in cloud access or configuration
Supply chains	A compromised third-party vendor becomes an entry point
Four steps to applying an attacker mindset
1. Identify the target: A person, system, organization, or piece of information.
2. Determine how it can be accessed: Identify information and potential entry points.
3. Evaluate attack vectors: Decide which pathways could be exploited.
4. Identify tools and methods: Consider how an attacker could use those pathways.
Defending attack vectors
- Educate users about current threats.
- Apply the principle of least privilege.
- Use appropriate security controls, such as antivirus software.
- Build diverse security teams with different perspectives.
5. Brute force attacks
A brute force attack is a trial-and-error technique used to discover credentials or other secret information by repeatedly trying possible combinations.
Four important techniques
1. Simple brute force
Repeatedly guesses combinations of usernames and passwords until a working combination is found.


2. Dictionary attack
Tries a list of commonly used passwords or credentials instead of every possible combination.


3. Reverse brute force
Starts with one commonly used or known password and tries it against multiple accounts or systems.


4. Credential stuffing
Uses usernames and passwords stolen from previous breaches to attempt access to accounts on other services.


Pass the hash: The course describes this as a specialized credential-stuffing technique that reuses stolen, unsalted hashed credentials to obtain an authenticated session. More generally, pass-the-hash attacks use a captured password hash directly in an authentication process rather than first recovering the original password.
Exhaustive key search is the related technique of trying possible encryption keys to recover encrypted information.
Brute force tools
Tool	Main association in your course
Aircrack-ng	Testing Wi-Fi security
Hashcat	Password hash recovery and auditing
John the Ripper	Password security auditing
Ophcrack	Password recovery using rainbow tables
THC Hydra	Testing authentication services against password-guessing attacks
These tools can be used for legitimate security testing on systems you own or have explicit permission to test.
6. Preventing brute force attacks
Organizations combine technical and managerial controls to reduce the likelihood of successful attacks.
A. Hashing and salting
- Hashing: Converts data into a fixed-length digest used for integrity checks and password verification.
- Salting: Adds a unique random value to a password before hashing it, making precomputed attacks less effective and identical passwords produce different salted hashes.
Salting does not make passwords impossible to crack, but it makes large-scale password cracking more difficult.
B. Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Requires two or more different authentication factors.
Example: A password plus a security key or authenticator-app code.
Even if a password is compromised, an attacker may still be unable to authenticate.
C. CAPTCHA
CAPTCHA stands for Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart.
It challenges users to complete a task intended to distinguish human users from automated programs.
Examples include:
- Entering distorted characters.
- Selecting specified objects in images.
CAPTCHA can make automated password guessing harder, though it is not a complete defense on its own.
D. Password policies
Organizations establish password rules and authentication procedures to reduce credential-related risks.
Examples from your course include:
- Minimum password-length requirements.
- Requirements for combinations of character types.
- Lockout policies that restrict repeated failed login attempts.
- Requirements to use unique passwords.
The course references NIST guidance for developing password policies: NIST Digital Identity Guidelines.
