1. Data and information security
Data is information that is translated, processed, or stored by a computer.
Information Security (InfoSec) is the practice of keeping data in all states away from unauthorized users.
Weak InfoSec can lead to:
- Identity theft
- Financial loss
- Reputational damage
Three states of data









1. Data in use
Data currently being accessed by one or more users.
Example: Reading your email on a laptop.












2. Data in transit
Data traveling from one point to another.
Example: Sending an email or message.












3. Data at rest
Data not currently being accessed, typically stored on a physical device.
Example: A file stored on a laptop's drive.



Important: Data's state depends on what it is doing, not just whether a device is switched on or off. Data stored in the cloud may remain accessible even when your phone is resting on a table.

2. Cloud computing
Cloud computing is an on-demand, massively scalable service hosted on shared infrastructure and accessible via the internet.
Cloud services help businesses:
- Start online operations more easily.
- Scale resources as needed.
- Lower costs.
- Connect with customers, employees, and business partners.
However, cloud adoption also creates cybersecurity challenges because data, applications, and infrastructure are accessed or managed through cloud environments.
Three types of cloud services
SaaS — Software as a Service

Users access applications through a web browser. The provider hosts, manages, and maintains the application's backend.
Examples from the course: Gmail, Slack, Zoom.


PaaS — Platform as a Service

Developers use online tools and environments to build, manage, and deploy applications. The provider maintains the underlying hardware and software platform.
Examples: Google App Engine, Heroku, VMware Cloud Foundry.


IaaS — Infrastructure as a Service

Customers access remote computing resources, including servers, storage, and networking, as needed.
Examples of cloud platforms: Google Cloud Platform and Microsoft Azure.


Quick way to remember
- SaaS: Use an application.
- PaaS: Build an application.
- IaaS: Access computing infrastructure.

3. Cloud security
Cloud security is the subfield of cybersecurity focused on protecting data, applications, and infrastructure in the cloud.
In traditional on-premises environments, an organization's internal security team is responsible for protecting its infrastructure. In cloud environments, responsibilities are shared.
Shared responsibility model
The shared responsibility model defines how security responsibilities are divided between the cloud service provider and the customer.
Customer responsibilities commonly include	Provider responsibilities commonly include
Identity and access management	Maintaining the underlying servers
Resource configuration	Maintaining provider-managed infrastructure
Data handling	Securing the systems the customer accesses, according to the service model
The exact division of responsibility depends on whether the service is SaaS, PaaS, or IaaS.
Key takeaway: Using a cloud provider does not mean the customer can ignore security. Customers must secure the resources and settings under their control.

4. Cloud security challenges
Know these four challenges from your reading:
1. Misconfiguration: Customers may leave default settings unchanged even when those settings do not meet their security requirements.
2. Cloud-native breaches: Misconfigured cloud services can make breaches more likely.
3. Access monitoring: Monitoring access may be difficult depending on the service and the customer's level of control.
4. Regulatory compliance: Organizations may need to meet specific legal or regulatory requirements.
Regulations mentioned in your course
- HIPAA
- PCI DSS
- GDPR
These are relevant to organizations that handle data subject to the respective requirements.
Cloud adoption creates new risks, increasing demand for professionals with cloud security skills.
