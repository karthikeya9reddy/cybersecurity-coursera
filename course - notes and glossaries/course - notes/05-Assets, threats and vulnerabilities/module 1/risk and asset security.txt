1. Security plans
Security is a team effort involving people, processes, and technology. Security culture means shared values and responsibilities across an organization, including employees, vendors, and customers.
Security plans prepare organizations for risks. They address risks such as information damage, disclosure, loss, device malfunctions, attacks, and human error.
Three elements of a security plan
1. Policies — Strategic

A set of rules that reduce risk and protect information. Policies establish the scope, objectives, and limitations of a security plan.
Example: Acceptable Use Policy (AUP), which defines acceptable use of corporate systems.


2. Standards — Tactical

References that inform how policies are set and establish a point of reference for security practices.
Example from the lecture: a password management standard specifying minimum password requirements.


3. Procedures — Step-by-step

Instructions for performing a specific security task.
Example: Steps for securely resetting a locked password.


Memory trick:
- Policy = what rules must be followed and why.
- Standard = the reference or requirement used to guide security practices.
- Procedure = how to perform a task.
Clear procedures promote accountability, consistency, and efficiency.

2. Compliance and regulations
Compliance is the process of adhering to internal standards and external regulations.
Organizations care about compliance to:
- Maintain trust and reputation.
- Protect people and information.
- Preserve data integrity and safety.
- Avoid fines, penalties, and lawsuits.
Regulations are rules established by a government or another authority to control how something is done.
Industries such as healthcare, energy, and finance may face strict regulatory requirements.
Important distinction
- Internal standards: Requirements an organization establishes for its operations.
- External regulations: Rules imposed by an external authority.
- Frameworks: Resources organizations can choose to use to manage cybersecurity risk.
The NIST CSF is a voluntary framework, not itself a regulation.

3. NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)
NIST stands for the National Institute of Standards and Technology.
The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is a voluntary framework consisting of standards, guidelines, and best practices to help organizations manage cybersecurity risk.
- Originally released in 2014.
- Initially developed to help protect critical infrastructure in the United States.
- Later adapted for public- and private-sector organizations of different sizes.
- Helps organizations identify assets, protect them, detect attacks, respond to incidents, and recover.
Three main components
The framework consists of:
1. Core
2. Tiers
3. Profiles
4. CSF Core — six functions
Your lecture initially describes five functions, which was the earlier version. The accompanying reading explains that Govern was added in February 2024, making six functions in the updated framework.

1. Identify
Understand assets, business needs, and cybersecurity risks.
Course connection: Asset inventories, asset classification, and risk assessment.




2. Protect
Implement safeguards to protect assets and information.




3. Detect
Identify possible cybersecurity attacks or incidents.




4. Respond
Take action when a cybersecurity incident occurs.




5. Recover
Restore affected systems and operations after an incident.




6. Govern
Establish cybersecurity risk strategy, oversight, and decision-making.
Added to the CSF in February 2024 to emphasize leadership's role.



Memory trick: Identify → Protect → Detect → Respond → Recover, with Govern providing leadership and oversight across cybersecurity risk management.

5. CSF Tiers
Tiers measure the sophistication of an organization's cybersecurity risk management practices and help assess its security posture.
The scale ranges from Tier 1 to Tier 4.
Tier	General meaning
Tier 1 — Partial	Limited or informal cybersecurity risk management.
Tier 2 — Risk Informed	Risk awareness informs practices, but they may not be consistently organization-wide.
Tier 3 — Repeatable	Risk management practices are established and consistently applied.
Tier 4 — Adaptive	Practices adapt and improve using experience and changing risks.
Your lecture calls Tier 1 “passive” and Tier 4 “adaptive.” For revision, remember the formal tier names shown above as well.
Tiers help organizations understand what's working and where improvement is needed.

6. CSF Profiles
Profiles help organizations describe and compare their cybersecurity posture against their business needs or desired outcomes.
They can be used to:
- Establish a baseline for a cybersecurity plan.
- Represent the current state of security operations.
- Identify gaps between current and desired security outcomes.
- Tailor cybersecurity practices to an industry or organization.
Memory trick: Core = functions; Tiers = sophistication; Profiles = current or desired cybersecurity posture.

7. Implementing the NIST CSF
The reading outlines four broad steps based on CISA guidance:
1. Create a current profile: Document current security operations and business needs.
2. Perform a risk assessment: Identify risks and assess whether operations meet business and regulatory requirements.
3. Analyze and prioritize gaps: Identify weaknesses that put assets at risk and decide which to address first.
4. Implement a plan of action: Take steps to achieve security goals and objectives.
The framework is flexible and can be applied across industries. Organizations should also consider changing threats, vulnerabilities, and risks.
