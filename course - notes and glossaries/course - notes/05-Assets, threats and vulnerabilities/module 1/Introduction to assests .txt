1. Security fundamentals
Key definitions
Term	Definition
Asset	Anything valuable to an organization that needs protection.
Threat	Any circumstance or event that can negatively impact an asset.
Vulnerability	A weakness that can be exploited by a threat.
Risk	Anything that can impact the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of an asset.
The CIA triad
- Confidentiality: Protect information from unauthorized disclosure.
- Integrity: Protect information from unauthorized alteration.
- Availability: Ensure information and systems remain accessible when needed.
Security plans analyze assets, threats, and vulnerabilities to assess risks to the CIA triad.
Risk calculation
\[
\text{Risk}=\text{Likelihood}\times\text{Impact}
\]

- Likelihood: How probable an adverse event is.
- Impact: How serious its consequences would be.
Organizations calculate risk to prevent costly disruptions, identify improvements, determine which risks can be tolerated, and prioritize critical assets.
Example — car tire:
- Asset: Car and its tire.
- Threat: A nail puncturing the tire.
- Vulnerability: The tire can be punctured by sharp objects.
- Risk: Arriving late to work.
- Risk reduction: Drive on a clean road to reduce the likelihood of a puncture.
2. Categories of threats and vulnerabilities
Threat categories
- Intentional: A malicious hacker targets a misconfigured application.
- Unintentional: An employee accidentally allows an unauthorized person into a restricted area.
Vulnerability categories
- Technical: Weaknesses in technology, such as misconfigured software.
- Human: Weaknesses involving people's actions or behavior.
Remember: a threat can exploit a vulnerability, potentially causing harm to an asset.

3. Asset management
Asset management is the process of tracking assets and the risks that affect them.
The principle: You can only protect the assets you know you have.
Why asset management matters
- Helps organizations keep track of important assets.
- Supports security planning and risk identification.
- Helps allocate security resources.
- Helps reduce IT costs.
- Supports compliance with legal regulations.
Asset inventory
An asset inventory is a catalog of assets that need to be protected.
It helps security teams know what assets exist and avoid losing track of important resources.
Examples of assets include:
Asset type	Examples
Digital	Customer data, financial records
Information systems	Networks, software
Physical	Buildings, equipment, supplies
Intangible	Brand reputation, intellectual property

4. Asset classification
Asset classification is the practice of labeling assets based on their sensitivity and importance to an organization.
To classify an asset, identify:
- What it is.
- Where it is.
- Who owns it.
- How important it is.
Common classification levels
From highest to lowest:
Restricted

Highest sensitivity
Highly sensitive, need-to-know information, such as payment or health information.



Confidential

Significant consequences if disclosed
Information intended for specific people, such as employees working on a particular project.



Internal-only

For internal use
Can be shared within the organization but should not be shared outside it.



Public

Lowest sensitivity
Can be shared with anyone without negative consequences to the organization.



Classification helps organizations prioritize security resources, manage risk, reduce IT costs, and comply with regulations.
Important: Classification schemes can vary between organizations. For example, some government organizations use confidential for their highest-sensitivity assets rather than restricted.
5. Challenges in asset classification
Ownership can be complicated. A company laptop belongs to the organization, but an employee might also store personal photos on it.
Information can have multiple sensitivity levels. A letter may contain a name that is public information and an address that should remain confidential.
Therefore, organizations must consider ownership, sensitivity, importance, and how information is used when classifying assets.
