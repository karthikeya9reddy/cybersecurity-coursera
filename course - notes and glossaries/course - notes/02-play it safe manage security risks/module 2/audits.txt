🔐 Security Audits — Complete Revision Notes
1. What is a Security Audit?
A security audit is a systematic review of an organization's:
- Security controls
- Policies
- Procedures
- Assets
- Security practices
against a defined set of expectations or criteria.
The purpose is to determine:
What is working? What is not? What risks exist? What needs to be fixed?

Audits help maintain and improve an organization's security posture.
The criteria can be:
Internal
- Company policies
- Procedures
- Security standards
- Best practices
External
- Laws
- Regulations
- Compliance requirements
- Industry standards
2. Why are Security Audits Important?
Audits help organizations:
- Identify security risks
- Find vulnerabilities and weaknesses
- Check whether controls are effective
- Identify compliance problems
- Improve security posture
- Create remediation plans
- Avoid regulatory penalties and fines
- Protect organizational assets and data
Think of an audit as a security health check.
3. Internal vs External Audit
Internal Audit
Performed within the organization, usually involving:
- Security team
- Security manager
- Compliance officer
- Other relevant employees
Entry-level analysts are more likely to assist with internal audits.
External Audit
Performed by an independent external party to evaluate whether the organization meets required standards, regulations, or other criteria.
4. Main Elements of an Internal Security Audit
Remember these 5 major stages:
1️⃣ Establish Scope & Goals
2️⃣ Conduct Risk Assessment
3️⃣ Complete Controls Assessment
4️⃣ Assess Compliance
5️⃣ Communicate Results
Let's break them down.
1️⃣ Establish Scope and Goals
Scope = What are we checking?
The scope defines what the audit will cover.
It can include:
- People
- Assets
- Systems
- Technologies
- Policies
- Procedures
- User permissions
- Network infrastructure
Goals = What do we want to achieve?
Examples:
- Improve security posture
- Implement NIST CSF functions
- Strengthen security controls
- Achieve compliance
- Reduce organizational risk
Easy way to remember:
Scope = WHERE/WHAT are we checking?
Goals = WHY are we checking it?

2️⃣ Conduct a Risk Assessment
A risk assessment identifies and evaluates:
- Threats
- Risks
- Vulnerabilities
- Potential impact
The organization asks:
What could go wrong, and how badly could it affect us?

Example:
An organization discovers that employees' laptops contain sensitive information but aren't properly protected.
Potential problem:
Vulnerability: Weak device security
↓
Threat: Attacker steals/compromises laptop
↓
Risk: Sensitive data is exposed
The assessment helps determine what security measures are necessary.
3️⃣ Controls Assessment
A controls assessment evaluates whether existing security controls are actually effective.
The analyst asks:
Are our current controls sufficient to protect our assets?

Controls are divided into three major categories.
🧑‍💼 Administrative Controls
Human/organizational controls.
Examples:
- Security policies
- Password policies
- Procedures
- Security awareness training
- Separation of duties
- Asset classification
💻 Technical Controls
Technology-based controls.
Examples:
- Firewalls
- MFA
- Encryption
- Antivirus
- IDS
- SIEM
🏢 Physical Controls
Controls that protect physical locations/assets.
Examples:
- Locks
- Security guards
- CCTV
- Motion detectors
- Access cards
- Fences
Remember:
Administrative = People & policies
Technical = Technology
Physical = Physical environment
4️⃣ Assess Compliance
Now determine whether the organization follows the laws, regulations, standards, and requirements that apply to it.
For example, a company:
- Operates in the EU
- Accepts credit-card payments
It may need to consider:
GDPR → protection of personal data
PCI DSS → protection of payment-card information
So the auditor asks:
"Are we actually complying with the requirements that apply to us?"

5️⃣ Communicate Results
After the audit, findings must be communicated to stakeholders.
The final report can contain:
- Audit scope
- Audit goals
- Identified risks
- Vulnerabilities
- Control weaknesses
- Compliance gaps
- Priority/severity
- Recommended improvements
- Mitigation/remediation actions
The purpose is to tell the organization:
Here's what we found, here's how serious it is, and here's what should be done.

🛠️ Audit Checklist
Before conducting an audit, organizations generally create an audit checklist.
Step 1 — Identify Scope
Determine:
- What assets will be assessed?
- What systems?
- What policies?
- What procedures?
- What technologies?
Example:
Is the firewall configured correctly?
Is PII protected?
Are physical assets secured?

Step 2 — Complete Risk Assessment
Evaluate risks related to:
- Assets
- Budget
- Controls
- Internal processes
- External regulations
Step 3 — Conduct the Audit
Actually evaluate the assets and controls identified in the scope.
Example:
Check whether employees actually follow the password policy.

Not just:
"Does a password policy exist?"

Step 4 — Create Mitigation Plan
A mitigation plan is a strategy for reducing identified risks.
Example:
Finding: Employees use weak passwords.
Mitigation:
- Strong password requirements
- MFA
- Password manager
- Security awareness training
The objective is to lower risk and potential negative consequences.
Step 5 — Communicate Results
Create a report containing:
Finding → Risk → Severity → Recommendation → Required action
Then communicate it to stakeholders.
🔗 How Frameworks, Controls, Compliance & Audits Connect
This is probably the most important concept from this section.
Think of it like this:
Frameworks
⬇️
Give you a structured approach
Controls
⬇️
Give you specific safeguards
Compliance
⬇️
Tells you what laws/regulations/requirements you must satisfy
Audit
⬇️
Checks whether everything is actually working and compliant
Example
A hospital wants to protect patient data.
Framework: NIST CSF
→ provides a structured security approach
Controls: MFA + encryption + access controls
→ actually protect the data
Compliance: HIPAA
→ legal requirement for protecting health information
Audit:
→ checks whether the hospital's controls and procedures actually meet its requirements.
🧠 Frameworks' Role in Audits
Frameworks such as:
- NIST CSF
- ISO/IEC 27001
help organizations prepare for audits.
They provide structured security practices that can help organizations:
- Identify risks
- Implement controls
- Improve security
- Demonstrate compliance
- Prepare for audits
⚠️ Important: Risk vs Audit
Don't confuse these.
Risk Assessment
What could go wrong?

Identifies and evaluates risks.
Security Audit
Are we actually doing what we're supposed to do?

Reviews controls, policies, procedures, and compliance against expectations.

🔥 Interview-Level Summary
If someone asks "What is a security audit?", you can say:
A security audit is a systematic review of an organization's security controls, policies, procedures, and practices against internal and external requirements. It identifies risks, control weaknesses, and compliance gaps and provides recommendations to improve the organization's security posture.

And if they ask "What would an entry-level security analyst do?":
An entry-level analyst may help review controls, analyze risks, verify compliance, examine evidence, document findings, and contribute to recommendations and audit reports.
