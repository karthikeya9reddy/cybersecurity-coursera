Course 6 – Incident Triage, Response and Recovery
PART 1: Incident Triage
1. What is triage?
Triage is the process of prioritizing incidents according to their level of importance or urgency.

Think of a hospital emergency department:
- A patient having a heart attack receives immediate attention.
- A patient with a broken finger may have to wait.
Hospitals have limited resources, so they prioritize patients based on urgency.

Security teams work similarly.

They receive many alerts every day, but not every alert requires an immediate response.

Triage helps security teams:
- Identify critical incidents.
- Prioritize urgent threats.
- Manage limited resources.
- Reduce response time.
- Prevent or minimize damage to an organization.
Example:
Incident	Priority
Active ransomware attack	High
Employee receives a suspicious phishing email	Depends on investigation
Multiple failed login attempts	Depends on context

An active ransomware attack generally requires a much more urgent response because it can affect confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA).
2. When does triage happen?
Triage begins after an incident is detected and an alert is generated.
Detection

IDS / SIEM detects suspicious activity

Alert generated

Security analyst receives the alert

Triage

Assess, prioritize, collect and analyze

Response / Escalation

Take appropriate action based on findings


3. Three steps of the triage process
Remember these three steps
Step 1: Receive and assess
The analyst receives an alert from an alerting system, such as an IDS.

The analyst reviews the alert to verify its validity and understand what triggered it.

Important questions:
Question	Purpose
Is it a false positive?	Determine whether a genuine threat exists
Was this alert triggered before?	Check whether it is new or recurring
Is it related to a known vulnerability?	Use existing knowledge to guide the response
What is its severity?	Understand the urgency of the response

False positive: An alert that incorrectly detects the presence of a threat.

Example: A security system flags legitimate employee activity as suspicious.

The analyst must investigate before deciding whether the alert is legitimate.
Step 2: Assign priority
Once an alert is assessed and verified as a genuine security issue, the analyst assigns an appropriate priority.

Priority depends on the incident's impact, size, and scope.

Your course identifies three important factors:

A. Functional impact

How does the incident affect the organization's business operations and services?

Example:
- Ransomware encrypts company computers.
- Employees cannot access essential systems.
- Business operations are disrupted.
B. Information impact

How does the incident affect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information?

Example:
- An attacker steals customer information.
- Sensitive data is exposed.
- Third-party users or organizations are affected.
C. Recoverability

How easily can the organization recover from the incident?

Consider:
- Can the affected systems be restored?
- How much time will recovery take?
- How many resources are required?
- Is recovery even possible?
Example: If proprietary information is stolen and publicly released, the organization may not be able to undo the disclosure.
Priority assessment – quick comparison
Factor	Main question
Functional impact	How badly are business operations affected?
Information impact	How badly is organizational data affected?
Recoverability	How easily can the organization recover?

Remember: An alert may already have an assigned severity or priority level, based on the organization's guidelines.
Step 3: Collect and analyze
This is the final step of triage.

The analyst performs a more comprehensive investigation.

Activities include:
- Gathering evidence from different sources.
- Reviewing system logs.
- Conducting external research.
- Analyzing suspicious activity.
- Documenting the investigation.
- Making an informed decision.
Depending on severity, the incident may be escalated to:
- Level 2 analyst: May have more advanced investigation knowledge and techniques.
- Manager: May oversee the incident and coordinate response efforts.
4. Adding context during an investigation
Never make assumptions based on a single alert.

Example: You receive an alert for a failed login attempt.

You should investigate:
1. Were there multiple failed login attempts?
2. Did the attempts occur outside normal working hours?
3. Did the login originate outside the organization's network?
4. Was anything else unusual associated with the alert?
These questions provide additional context.

Key lesson: Context helps prevent incomplete or incorrect conclusions.
5. Benefits of triage
A. Resource management
- Focuses resources on urgent threats.
- Prevents wasting time on low-priority alerts.
- Helps reduce response time.
B. Standardized approach
- Establishes a consistent incident-handling process.
- Uses documentation and playbooks.
- Ensures alerts are properly assessed and validated.
- Helps ensure only valid alerts are escalated for further investigation.

PART 2: Containment, Eradication and Recovery
This is the third phase of the incident response lifecycle.

The three steps are closely connected:
1. CONTAINMENT

Limit further damage

2. ERADICATION

Remove the threat completely

3. RECOVERY

Restore normal operations


These steps align with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework's Respond and Recover functions.
1. Containment
Containment is the act of limiting and preventing additional damage caused by an incident.

Organizations document their containment strategies in incident response plans.

Example: Malware infects one computer in a company's network.

The security team isolates the affected computer by disconnecting it from the network.

Why?
- Prevents malware from spreading.
- Limits the incident to the affected system.
- Reduces additional damage.
Remember: Containment stops the incident from spreading or causing further damage. It does not necessarily remove the threat.
2. Eradication
Eradication involves completely removing the incident elements from all affected systems.

After containment, the security team works to eliminate the threat.

Examples from your course:
- Performing vulnerability tests.
- Applying patches to vulnerabilities related to the threat.
The goal is to remove the elements responsible for the incident.

Remember: Containment = Stop the damage. Eradication = Remove the threat.
3. Recovery
Recovery is the process of returning affected systems to normal operations.

Recovery actions include:
- Reimaging affected systems.
- Resetting passwords.
- Adjusting network configurations.
- Modifying firewall rules.
Example:

After a ransomware incident:
1. Affected systems are contained.
2. Malware and related vulnerabilities are addressed.
3. Systems are restored and brought back into normal operation.
Remember: Recovery = Restore normal business operations.
Comparison of the three
Phase	Main purpose	Example
Containment	Limit damage	Isolate infected computer
Eradication	Remove threat	Remove malware and patch vulnerabilities
Recovery	Restore operations	Reimage and restore systems

Important: The incident response lifecycle is cyclical. Security teams may need to revisit previous phases when new evidence or related incidents appear.

PART 3: Business Continuity Planning (BCP)
1. What is business continuity planning?
A security incident can disrupt normal business operations.

For example:
- Ransomware can make company systems inaccessible.
- Data breaches can expose sensitive information.
- Infrastructure attacks can disrupt essential services.
Prolonged disruption can cause:
- Legal damage.
- Financial losses.
- Reputational damage.
Organizations use business continuity planning to minimize these disruptions.

Business Continuity Plan (BCP): A document that outlines procedures to sustain business operations during and after a significant disruption.

Its purpose is to ensure critical business functions can continue or be quickly restored.
Example: Ransomware in a hospital







esslinger-zeitung.de


Imagine ransomware encrypts a hospital's systems.

Consequences:
- Medical records become inaccessible.
- Doctors may not access patient information.
- Healthcare services may be disrupted.
- Patient safety could be affected.
A BCP helps the hospital maintain essential services despite the disruption.

Important: Entry-level security analysts are not typically responsible for developing and testing BCPs, but they should understand their purpose.
2. BCP vs Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)
Important comparison
These two plans are related, but they are not the same.
BCP	DRP
Business Continuity Plan	Disaster Recovery Plan
Focuses on sustaining business operations	Focuses on recovering information systems
Helps critical business functions continue during and after disruption	Restores systems after a major disaster
Broader business operations focus	IT systems recovery focus

Example:

A company experiences a major data center failure.
- BCP: How can the company continue serving customers?
- DRP: How can the company restore its information systems?
Remember:

BCP = Keep the business running.

DRP = Recover IT systems.

3. Resilience
Resilience is the ability to prepare for, respond to, and recover from disruptions.

Organizations design resilient systems so that they can continue delivering services despite disruptions.

One example is site resilience.
Site resilience
Site resilience helps ensure the availability of:
- Networks.
- Data centers.
- Other infrastructure.
Organizations use different types of recovery sites to maintain availability when their primary location experiences a disruption.

There are three types.
A. Hot site







optage.co.jp


A hot site is a fully operational facility that duplicates an organization's primary environment.

Characteristics:
- Fully operational.
- Ready for immediate activation.
- Can take over when the primary site fails.
Example: A company has a backup data center that is already operational and can be activated immediately.
B. Warm site







hgcit.co.uk


A warm site contains an updated and configured version of the organization's environment, but it is not fully operational or immediately available like a hot site.

Characteristics:
- Infrastructure is prepared.
- Requires some additional work to become operational.
- Can be made operational relatively quickly.
C. Cold site







dpr.com


A cold site is a backup facility equipped with some of the infrastructure required to operate an organization's site.

Characteristics:
- Not ready for immediate use.
- Requires additional work and setup.
- Takes longer to become operational.
Hot vs Warm vs Cold site
Very important for revision
Feature	Hot site	Warm site	Cold site
Operational status	Fully operational	Partially prepared	Basic infrastructure
Readiness	Immediate	Requires some work	Requires more work
Recovery speed	Fastest	Intermediate	Slowest
Preparation	Highest	Moderate	Lowest

Easy memory trick:
- Hot = Ready to go.
- Warm = Almost ready.
- Cold = Needs preparation.
