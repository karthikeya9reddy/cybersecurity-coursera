Course 6 – Documentation and Playbooks
1. What is documentation?
Documentation is any form of recorded content used for a specific purpose.

Security teams use documentation to:
- Support security investigations.
- Record actions taken during incidents.
- Complete tasks and follow procedures.
- Communicate findings to other team members.
- Maintain evidence for audits and legal proceedings.
For example, when a security alert is triggered, analysts need documentation explaining:
- What the alert means.
- What severity to assign.
- What could cause a false positive.
- How to verify whether the alert is legitimate.
- What actions should be taken next.
Without documentation, knowledge stays with individual analysts. This makes it difficult to train new employees or scale a SOC team.
2. Three major benefits of documentation
1
Transparency

Creates a record of what happened, who performed an action, and when it happened.

2
Standardization

Establishes consistent guidelines, processes, and procedures that everyone follows.

3
Clarity

Makes roles, responsibilities, instructions, and decisions easy to understand.

A. Transparency
Transparency means maintaining records so that relevant information about activities and decisions can be accessed.

Why is it important?
- Provides a record of actions taken during an incident.
- Helps demonstrate compliance with regulations and internal processes.
- Provides evidence for security insurance claims.
- Supports regulatory investigations and legal proceedings.
- Makes it easier to review previous actions.
Example: An analyst documents why an alert was escalated, what evidence was collected, and who received that evidence.

This creates an audit trail.

Important term – Audit trail: A chronological record of activities and actions that allows an organization to review what happened.
B. Standardization
Standardization means establishing a common set of guidelines, standards, processes, and procedures that everyone follows.

Benefits:
- Maintains consistent quality of work.
- Makes processes repeatable.
- Supports continuous improvement.
- Helps transfer knowledge between employees.
- Makes onboarding new team members easier.
Example: An organization creates an incident response plan that explains what employees must do during each stage of incident response.

Everyone follows the same documented process instead of making up their own approach.

Standards: References that inform how an organization should establish its policies.

For example, NIST provides security frameworks that organizations can use to improve their security measures.
C. Clarity
Clarity means making information easy to understand so that people know what they need to do and why.

Benefits:
- Defines roles and responsibilities.
- Explains how to complete tasks.
- Reduces uncertainty and confusion.
- Helps analysts understand why an alert was escalated or closed.
Example: A playbook provides an analyst with step-by-step instructions for responding to a ransomware incident.

Instead of guessing what to do, the analyst follows the documented instructions.

3. Chain of custody
Important concept
Chain of custody is the process of documenting the possession and control of evidence throughout the incident lifecycle.

In cybersecurity, evidence may include:
- Hard drives.
- Disk images.
- Log files.
- Network packet captures.
- Other digital forensic data.
The purpose is to maintain a record of who handled the evidence, when they handled it, where it went, and why it was transferred.
Why is chain of custody important?
During an investigation, evidence may eventually be used in legal proceedings.

The security team must demonstrate that:
- Evidence was properly collected.
- Evidence was not improperly modified.
- Every transfer was documented.
- The evidence can be trusted.
Example: Hard drive forensic investigation
Imagine a compromised hard drive needs to be examined by a digital forensics team.







ibcourses.ru


The process described in your course:
1. Aisha collects the hard drive. She ensures that it is write-protected so its data cannot be edited or erased.
2. Aisha calculates a cryptographic hash. She calculates and records the hash of an image of the hard drive.
3. Aisha transfers it to Colin. The transfer is recorded in the chain of custody form.
4. Colin examines the evidence. He then sends it to Nav, and that transfer is recorded.
5. Nav sends it to Arman. This transfer is also recorded.
Every time evidence changes hands, the chain of custody form must be updated.
What is the role of hashing?
A cryptographic hash function produces a fixed-length value from data.

In digital forensics, the original hash can be compared with a newly calculated hash to check whether the data has changed.

For example:
- Original hash: A1B2C3...
- Later hash: A1B2C3... → matching values suggest the data has not changed.
- Later hash: X9Y8Z7... → different values indicate that the data has changed.
Illustrative hashes only.

Remember: Hashing helps detect changes to evidence. It does not itself prevent someone from modifying the evidence. Write protection helps prevent modifications to the original storage device.
What information does a chain of custody form contain?
There is no single standard template, but common elements include:
Element	Information recorded
Evidence description	Details identifying the evidence
Identification information	Location, hostname, MAC address, IP address, etc.
Custody log	Names of people transferring and receiving evidence
Date and time	When evidence was collected or transferred
Purpose	Why the evidence was transferred

Broken chain of custody
Broken chain of custody occurs when there are inconsistencies in the collection or logging of evidence.

Examples:
- A transfer happened but was not recorded.
- The receiving person's name is missing.
- The date or time is incorrect.
- There is an unexplained gap in evidence handling.
Why is it dangerous?

A broken chain of custody can affect the:
- Integrity of evidence.
- Reliability of evidence.
- Accuracy of evidence.
This can make the evidence difficult to trust or use in court.

Quick example: If Aisha gives a hard drive to Colin but nobody records the transfer, there is a gap in the evidence's documented history.

4. Documentation best practices
Security professionals should follow three important practices when creating documentation.
A. Know your audience
Always consider who will read your document.

Different audiences have different levels of technical knowledge.
Audience	Documentation style
SOC manager	Technical details, alerts, evidence, severity
CEO	Business impact, risks, financial consequences
Security analyst	Technical instructions and investigation steps

Example:

For a SOC manager:

"An endpoint generated repeated outbound connections to a suspicious IP address."

For a CEO:

"A company computer may have communicated with a malicious server, potentially exposing company information."

Same incident, but different explanations depending on the audience.
B. Be concise
Documentation should be clear and to the point.

Why?
- Long documents can discourage people from reading them.
- Important information should be easy to find.
- Readers should understand the purpose immediately.
Example – Executive summary

An executive summary presents the major facts and findings of an incident at the beginning of a final report.

It should be brief and easy to skim.
C. Update regularly
Security threats and regulatory requirements constantly change.

Documentation must be reviewed and updated regularly.

Examples:
- New vulnerabilities are discovered.
- Attack techniques evolve.
- Security procedures change.
- Regulations may be updated.
- Incident reviews may reveal gaps in existing procedures.
Important: The post-incident phase is a good opportunity to identify improvements and update documentation.

5. Security playbooks
Important concept
A playbook is a manual that provides detailed instructions about operational actions.

In cybersecurity, playbooks guide security analysts through the steps required to respond to a particular incident.

Think of a playbook as a travel itinerary.

A travel itinerary tells you:
- Where to go.
- What to do.
- When to do it.
- How to move between destinations.
Similarly, a security playbook tells an analyst:
- What to investigate.
- Which evidence to collect.
- What actions to perform.
- What to do next.
Why are playbooks important?
Incident response can be unpredictable and stressful. Security teams must respond quickly and effectively.

Playbooks provide structure and order.

Benefits:
- Reduce guesswork and uncertainty.
- Provide step-by-step instructions.
- Help analysts act quickly.
- Maintain consistency across the team.
- Reduce mistakes during stressful situations.
- Help analysts complete all required steps.
Playbooks and checklists
Playbooks may contain checklists to help analysts remember every required action.

For example, a ransomware response checklist might include:
- Identify affected systems.
- Collect relevant logs and evidence.
- Investigate suspicious activity.
- Follow the organization's containment procedures.
- Document actions taken.
The specific actions depend on the organization's playbook.

7. Three types of playbooks
There are three types of playbooks based on how much automation is involved.
1. Non-automated playbook
Manual
All steps are performed manually by a security analyst.

Example: An analyst manually identifies suspicious traffic, collects logs, and investigates a DDoS attack.

2. Automated playbook
Automated
Tasks in the incident response process are performed automatically by configured tools.

Examples:
- Categorizing incident severity.
- Gathering evidence.
Benefits:
- Reduces manual work.
- Can reduce time to resolution.
SOAR and SIEM tools can be configured to automate playbooks.

3. Semi-automated playbook
Human + automation
Combines automated tasks with actions performed by a human analyst.

Example:
- Automation gathers evidence and enriches an alert.
- An analyst reviews the evidence and decides what action to take.
Benefits:
- Increases productivity.
- Reduces repetitive work.
- Saves analyst time.
- Can decrease time to resolution.

Comparison table
Type	Who performs the work?	Example
Non-automated	Human analyst	Manually collect and analyze logs
Automated	Tools	Automatically categorize severity
Semi-automated	Human + tools	Tool gathers evidence; analyst investigates

Remember the difference:
- Non-automated = Human does the work.
- Automated = System does the configured work.
- Semi-automated = Both work together.
Role of SIEM and SOAR
You have already studied SIEM in the detection and analysis section.
- SIEM: Collects and analyzes security event data, helping analysts detect suspicious activity.
- SOAR: Helps orchestrate and automate security operations and response tasks.
Both can be configured to support automated playbooks.

8. Maintaining and updating playbooks
Playbooks should not be created once and forgotten.

Security threats constantly evolve, so playbooks must be maintained and updated regularly.

When should a security team update them?

Post-incident activity phase is a particularly good time.

After an incident is resolved, the team can review:
- What worked well?
- What did not work?
- Were any steps missing?
- Did analysts face confusion?
- Could any tasks be automated?
- Do existing procedures need changes?
The team can then update its playbooks to improve future incident responses.
