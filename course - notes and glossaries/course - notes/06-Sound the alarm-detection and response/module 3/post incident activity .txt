Course 6 – Post-Incident Activity
1. What is post-incident activity?
Post-incident activity is the process of reviewing an incident to identify areas for improvement in incident handling.

It happens after the security team has completed:
- Containment
- Eradication
- Recovery
But the team's work doesn't completely end there.

The organization reviews the incident to understand:
- What happened?
- How did it happen?
- How effectively did the team respond?
- What mistakes or gaps were identified?
- How can similar incidents be prevented?
Main objective: Learn from previous incidents and improve future incident response.
2. Lessons learned meeting (Post-mortem)
Important concept
A lessons learned meeting, also known as a post-mortem, is a meeting held after a major incident to review the incident, evaluate response actions, and identify areas for improvement.
When is it conducted?
- Generally after an incident has been successfully remediated.
- Should be scheduled no later than two weeks after the incident.
- Major incidents, such as ransomware attacks, should have a dedicated meeting.
- Multiple meetings may be scheduled if the incident is complex.
Not every small incident requires a dedicated lessons learned meeting. The size and severity determine whether one is necessary.
Who participates?
All relevant parties involved in the incident response process.

For example:
- Security analysts.
- Incident response team.
- IT administrators.
- Security managers.
- Other affected departments.
Questions discussed during the meeting
Question	Purpose
What happened?	Understand the incident
What time did it happen?	Establish the timeline
Who discovered it?	Identify how it was detected
How did it get contained?	Review containment actions
What actions were taken for recovery?	Evaluate recovery efforts
What could have been done differently?	Identify improvements

Important principle: No blame culture
The purpose of a lessons learned meeting is learning and improvement, not assigning blame.

For example:

An employee clicks a phishing link, resulting in malware spreading through the organization.

Instead of blaming the employee, the security team should investigate:
- Why did the phishing email reach the employee?
- Were email security controls insufficient?
- Was security awareness training effective?
- Could detection have happened earlier?
The objective is to identify weaknesses and prevent recurrence.
Meeting preparation
Before the meeting, organizers should prepare:

1. Meeting agenda

A document containing the topics to be discussed.

It ensures that participants arrive prepared.

2. Meeting roles
Role	Responsibility
Moderator	Leads and facilitates the discussion
Scribe	Records meeting notes

3. Recommendations from lessons learned meetings
The meeting should produce a list of prioritized actions or actionable recommendations.

These recommendations help improve incident handling and the organization's overall security posture.

Examples of improvements:
- Fix errors in incident response procedures.
- Identify gaps in security processes.
- Improve ineffective security controls.
- Update existing playbooks.
- Introduce new security tools and technologies.
Example:

A ransomware incident revealed that the organization took too long to isolate infected computers.

Recommendation:
- Update the ransomware response playbook.
- Improve endpoint detection capabilities.
- Establish a faster isolation procedure.
The goal is to ensure that lessons learned are actually implemented.

4. Final report
Very important
A final report is documentation that provides a comprehensive review of a security incident.

It records the incident's details, timeline, investigation, findings, and recommendations for future prevention.
Purpose of a final report
- Provides a complete record of the incident.
- Documents the sequence of events.
- Explains the investigation and actions taken.
- Communicates findings to relevant people.
- Helps during lessons learned meetings.
- Provides recommendations to prevent similar incidents.
Important: The final report is the main reference document used during the lessons learned meeting.
Four common elements of a final report
Your course identifies four important components.
A. Executive summary
A high-level summary containing the most important facts and findings.

It should communicate:
- What happened?
- What was affected?
- What was the impact?
- What were the major findings?
It is especially useful for executives and non-technical readers.
B. Timeline
A detailed chronological record of events related to the incident.

It includes timestamps showing the sequence of events.

Example:
Time	Event
09:00 AM	Suspicious email received
09:15 AM	Employee clicks malicious link
09:20 AM	Security alert generated
09:30 AM	Analyst investigates alert
09:45 AM	Affected system isolated
11:00 AM	Eradication activities begin

Illustrative timeline.
C. Investigation
A compilation of the actions taken during the detection and analysis of the incident.

It can include:
- Alerts investigated.
- Logs examined.
- Evidence collected.
- Network traffic analyzed.
- Findings discovered.
Example: A packet capture (PCAP) reveals suspicious network communication that helps analysts understand what happened.
D. Recommendations
A list of suggested actions to prevent similar incidents in the future.

Examples:
- Improve security controls.
- Update incident response playbooks.
- Patch vulnerable systems.
- Improve employee security awareness.
- Introduce new security tools.
Final report structure – quick table
Component	Purpose
Executive summary	High-level overview
Timeline	Chronological sequence of events
Investigation	Actions, evidence, and findings
Recommendations	Future prevention and improvements

5. Consider the audience when writing a final report
Final reports may be read by different audiences.

For example:
Audience	What they need
CEO / Executives	Business impact, risks, major findings
SOC Manager	Incident details, response effectiveness, recommendations
Security Analysts	Technical investigation, logs, evidence, timeline

Remember: Avoid overwhelming non-technical readers with unnecessary technical details.

The report should communicate important findings in language the intended audience understands.

7. Quick revision sheet
Post-incident activity
- Fourth and final phase of the NIST Incident Response Lifecycle.
- Reviews incidents to identify improvements.
- Focuses on learning and preventing recurrence.

Lessons learned meeting
- Also called post-mortem.
- Generally scheduled within two weeks after remediation.
- Includes relevant incident participants.
- Reviews what happened and how the response worked.
- Focuses on learning, not blaming.
- Moderator leads; scribe records notes.
- Produces prioritized recommendations.

Final report
- Comprehensive review of an incident.
- Main reference document for lessons learned meetings.
- Includes four common components:
  1. Executive summary.
  2. Timeline.
  3. Investigation.
  4. Recommendations.
- Must be tailored to its audience.
